### **PREFACE.**

It is now some twenty years since my attention was first directed, by a series of accidents which it would be tedious to relate, to that obscure and much-ridiculed province of inquiry which, for want of a more precise term, I have ventured to call the Borderland. The Borderland is that shadowy frontier lying between the mapped territories of physiology, physics, and moral philosophy, where phenomena present themselves which appear to belong to none of these kingdoms, yet which impinge upon all. In traversing this region, the explorer is compelled to abandon the comfortable certainties of the laboratory and the school, and to hold his judgment in suspense, as a lantern whose beam penetrates but a few feet into an encompassing darkness.

The work now offered to the public is not a systematic text-book—for the subject does not admit of a Euclid—but rather a record of patient observation and cautious induction, conducted as far as possible under conditions of experimental rigour. I have endeavoured to write with the calmness of a man of science, yet without that arrogant materialism which dismisses whole classes of facts because they do not fit the current hypotheses of the hour. The age in which we live has witnessed an extraordinary revival of claims which the eighteenth century supposed itself to have buried for ever under the tombstone of common sense. Animal magnetism, clairvoyance, thought-transference, the physical phenomena of the séance, and even the materialisation of spirit-forms—all these are once more thrust upon the attention of serious men, not by the ravings of the superstitious, but by the sworn testimony of physicians, chemists, and astronomers whose competence is beyond dispute. It is the duty of philosophy to sift this evidence, to separate the gold from the dross, and, where the gold is genuine, to assay its quality and determine its lawful place in the economy of Nature.

I am not unmindful of the objections that will be raised. I shall be accused, on the one hand, by the dogmatic materialist, of credulity and of lending the authority of my name to the follies of the séance-room. On the other hand, I shall be taxed by the uncritical spiritualist with a coldness amounting to treason, because I refuse to accept every manifestation as a message from the blessed dead. To both parties I reply, that the true disciple of Bacon follows wherever the facts lead, and owns no allegiance save to Truth. If the phenomena examined in these pages be real—and I am satisfied that many of them are as real as the stars that shine above us—then the universe is a stranger and a grander place than materialism has dreamed, and man possesses, in his own organism, faculties which connect him with a plane of being transcending the gross limitations of the flesh. This is not a conclusion to be embraced lightly; it is one to be tested by every engine of sceptical analysis. That testing I have endeavoured, however imperfectly, to perform.

A large portion of the matter here presented is drawn from my own notebooks, kept during a long series of sittings with mesmerists, sensitives, and mediums of every grade. Where I have availed myself of the labours of fellow-investigators—such as Professor William Crookes, Mr. Alfred Russel Wallace, Dr. Frederic Myers, and the late Professor de Morgan—I have scrupulously acknowledged the debt. I have also included, in an Appendix, a selection of cases attested by witnesses whose veracity I have no reason to doubt, and which appear to me to meet the legitimate demands of scientific evidence.

It remains only to express my hope that this volume may fall into the hands of readers who are willing to follow, without prejudice, a train of reasoning that leads somewhat beyond the limits of the conventional philosophy. If the work provoke the orthodox scientist to inquiry, and at the same time induce the zealous spiritualist to demand better proofs than he has hitherto been content to accept, I shall consider my labour amply rewarded.

ALASTAIR THORNE.

EDINBURGH,  
*October, 1887.*

### **INTRODUCTION.**

#### **The Revival of Wonder.**

In the year of grace eighteen hundred and eighty-seven, it requires no small measure of courage to confess publicly that one has been engaged for a quarter of a century in the investigation of what are vulgarly styled “ghosts” and “table-turnings.” The man of science who ventures his reputation upon such an enterprise must be prepared to be called a fool by the physicists, a dupe by the physiologists, and an infidel by the theologians. Yet the work has been done, and done by men whose names are illustrious enough to outshine the ridicule of a generation. Sir Isaac Newton, were he living to-day, would not be ashamed to speculate upon the nature of a force which moves matter without the impact of ponderable bodies; and the same Michael Faraday, who exposed so many trickeries, never denied that there might exist natural agencies as yet unknown to the laboratory.

The last half-century has witnessed, in every department of thought, a remarkable crumbling of dogmatic barriers. Geology has extended the history of our planet by millions of years; biology has unveiled a world of microscopic life whose fecundity and complexity confound the imagination; and the spectroscope has taught us that the stars are made of the same elements as our own earth. In the face of such revelations, it becomes the merest provincialism to assert that we have already catalogued all the forces of Nature, and that nothing remains to be discovered save the application of known laws. It is in this spirit of intellectual humility, rather than of credulity, that I approach the phenomena which form the subject of the present volume.

I use the term “Borderland” advisedly. A borderland is a region claimed by two sovereignties, but belonging wholly to neither. The facts which I am about to describe lie upon the borders of physiology, because they involve the human organism in states which the physiologist has not yet explained; upon the borders of physics, because they seem to manifest a force that moves ponderable objects without the interposition of muscle or mechanical lever; and upon the borders of psychology, because they thrust upon our notice modes of consciousness which transcend the ordinary limitations of the individual ego. If I am asked whether these phenomena are “physical” or “spiritual,” I reply that the antithesis is a false one. The physical and the spiritual are but two aspects of one reality, viewed from different sides of the veil. A force which raises a table from the floor, and which is directed by an intelligence that answers questions, is, in its lower aspect, a measurable stress; in its higher aspect, it is a manifestation of mind. There is no more contradiction in this than there is in the fact that my arm is moved by my will, yet obeys the laws of muscular contraction.

The revival of interest in these matters, which is one of the distinguishing marks of the Victorian era, may be dated, for convenience, from the foundation of the Society for Dialectical Studies in London, and the subsequent formation of the Society for Psychical Research in 1882. These bodies have brought to the examination of the supernormal a discipline of method and a severity of verification which were wholly absent from the early annals of mesmerism and spiritualism. The result is that we now possess a mass of evidence, sifted and cross-sifted by lawyers and men of business, which places certain phenomena—thought-transference, for instance, and the movement of objects without contact—upon a footing of scientific probability, if not of absolute demonstration. It is this residue of well-attested fact that I propose to examine in the following chapters.

I must beg the reader to distinguish carefully between the *facts* and the *theories* by which men have sought to explain them. That a table moves when no one is touching it, in the presence of certain peculiarly constituted individuals, is a fact established by testimony as good as that which supports the existence of the planet Neptune. The assertion that the table is moved by the spirits of the dead is a theory, to be weighed in the balance of evidence like any other hypothesis. I confess that, for my own part, the spiritistic interpretation appears to be the most comprehensive, and the one which best harmonises with the deeper intuitions of mankind; but I am far from setting it up as an article of dogma. The true inquirer keeps his mind open, and is content to accumulate facts, leaving to a future generation the task of constructing a complete theory.

In these pages I shall take the reader by the hand, and lead him, step by step, from the simpler to the more complex manifestations. We shall begin with that modification of the nervous force which is called animal magnetism—a force which, whatever be its ultimate nature, is as real as electricity, and as obedient to experimental manipulation. We shall then pass to the phenomena of clairvoyance, trance, physical mediumship, materialisation, and thought-transference. At each stage, I shall offer such comments and criticisms as occur to a mind trained in the exact sciences, and I shall indicate the precautions which I have found necessary to exclude the ever-present possibility of fraud. Finally, I shall attempt a philosophical synthesis—tentative and incomplete, no doubt, but such as may serve to map the general outlines of this uncharted territory.

One word more. In dealing with matters which touch so nearly the deepest affections of the human heart—the hope of continued existence, the possibility of communion with those who have passed beyond the grave—it is impossible to preserve the dry and impersonal tone of a chemical treatise. Nor do I think it desirable. The whole man must be brought to the examination of a problem which concerns the whole man. Science, properly understood, is not a cold abstraction; it is the disciplined effort of the human spirit to read the thoughts of God. If, in the course of this inquiry, I am led to the conclusion that the universe is a moral order, ruled by an Intelligence that answers the cry of its creatures, and that death is but the passage from one chamber of that order to another, I shall not apologise for the warmth with which I express such a conviction. The truth, whatever it be, is a sacred thing.

Let us proceed, then, with courage and with caution, into the Borderland.

### **CHAPTER I.**

#### **ANIMAL MAGNETISM AND THE ODIC FORCE.**

The modern history of our subject commences, appropriately enough, in the year 1778, when Dr. Friedrich Anton Mesmer, a Swabian physician, arrived in Paris and began the series of cures and controversies which have rendered his name immortal. Mesmer’s fundamental thesis was simple. He maintained that there exists, throughout the universe, a subtle, all-pervading fluid, which he called “animal magnetism,” and that upon the equilibrium of this fluid in the human body depends the health of the individual. By the application of magnets, or, as he later discovered, by simple “passes” of the hands, this fluid could be directed, accumulated, and redistributed, so as to produce effects which ranged from a gentle drowsiness to violent convulsions, and which frequently terminated in the cure of obstinate diseases.

The medical faculty of Paris, after a solemn investigation, condemned Mesmer as a charlatan, and the Academy of Sciences declared that the magnetic fluid had no existence. Posterity, however, has been more just. Whatever we may think of Mesmer’s theory, the facts which he unearthed are as solid as the granite of his native mountains. The mesmeric state—we now call it the hypnotic state—is an undeniable reality. The power of one human being to throw another into a trance, to control his actions, to abolish his pain, and to elicit faculties which are dormant in the normal condition, is a truth admitted by the most sceptical neurologists of our own day. Mesmer’s fluid has been banished from the textbooks; the phenomena which the fluid was invoked to explain have remained.

Mesmer’s pupil, the Marquis de Puységur, made the next great step. In 1784, while magnetising a young peasant named Victor Race, Puységur observed that the subject, instead of falling into convulsions, passed into a quiet sleep, during which he appeared to be in possession of a new and heightened consciousness. He answered questions intelligently, manifested an intimate knowledge of his own malady and its proper treatment, and, on waking, retained no memory of what had occurred. Puységur called this condition “artificial somnambulism,” and the name is as good as any. It is, in fact, a state of profound rapport between the operator and the subject, in which the latter reads the thoughts of the former, and, in certain remarkable cases, appears to perceive objects and events removed by distance and by time from the ordinary channels of sense.

I shall not detain the reader with a detailed history of the magnetic movement, fascinating though it be. The names of Deleuze, Bertrand, Teste, and Elliotson will always be honoured by those who value the unbroken tradition of a true science. In our own country, the labours of Dr. James Braid, who stripped the mesmeric procedure of its superfluous mystifications and showed that the state could be produced by the mere fixation of the eyes upon a bright object, have placed the induction of trance upon a firm physiological basis. Braid’s term “hypnotism” has largely supplanted the older “mesmerism,” but the change of name has not altered the substance. The operator’s will, the “passes,” and the fixed gaze all produce a condition of the brain and nervous system which renders the subject exquisitely sensitive to suggestion, and which opens a door, so to speak, into the deeper strata of consciousness.

But what of the fluid itself? Is there, after all, a veritable *emanation* from the human body, perceptible to the senses of certain specially organised persons, and capable of being guided by the will? The question received a new and startling answer from the experiments of Baron Karl von Reichenbach, a German chemist of high distinction, who published his results in the year 1845 under the title of *Researches on Magnetism, Electricity, Heat, and Light in their Relations to the Vital Force*. Reichenbach discovered that a number of individuals, whom he called “sensitives,” could perceive, in the dark, a luminous emanation proceeding from the poles of magnets, from crystals, and, most markedly, from the human hand. This emanation, which he named the *odic force* (after Odin, the All-Father), presented itself to the sensitives as a delicate flame, usually blue or violet at the positive pole, and reddish-yellow at the negative. It was accompanied by sensations of warmth or coolness, and, when drawn through the air by the breath or by a current, it adhered, like a vapour, to the surfaces it touched.

I confess that, when I first read Reichenbach’s treatise, I was disposed to dismiss it as the fantasy of an overheated brain. But I determined to repeat his experiments, and I have been compelled to modify my scepticism. In a series of trials conducted in my own laboratory, with every precaution against collusion and self-deception, I have obtained results which are, to my mind, perfectly conclusive. I took two powerful bar-magnets, wrapped in identical coverings of black velvet, so that no visible difference could be detected between them. I then placed them, one after the other, before a young woman of nervous temperament who had never heard of Reichenbach, and who had no notion of the properties of a magnet. When her hand was brought near the north-seeking pole, she immediately exclaimed, “There is a cool wind coming from that one.” When the south-seeking pole was presented, she said, “That feels warm, like a glow.” The experiment was repeated twenty times, with variations of position and with dummy magnets, and in every case she correctly distinguished the poles, even when her eyes were bandaged and the magnets held at a distance of six inches from her skin. I was myself entirely unable to perceive any sensible effluence; but I am not upon that account justified in denying the faculty of those who are more delicately organised. The philosopher who refuses to believe in a sensation which he does not share must, to be consistent, deny the existence of colour-blindness, or of the musical ear.

The odic force, whatever its ultimate nature, appears to be intimately connected with the nervous energy of the living body. The hands of a healthy and vigorous man radiate a copious supply; the hands of the aged and the sick are comparatively inert. A “pass” made with the right hand sends forth a stream of odic influence which the sensitive sees as a trailing scarf of violet flame; a pass with the left hand yields a complementary hue. The force is capable of being stored in water, in glass, and in porous substances, just as heat is stored by a brick. Reichenbach’s sensitives declared that a glass of water which had been held for a few minutes in the hand of a strong magnetiser tasted distinctly “odic”—a faint, metallic, and not unpleasant flavour—while the same water, uncharged, had no such taste.

It is impossible, in the present state of our knowledge, to determine the precise relation of the odic force to the imponderable agents already recognised by science. Is it a new and distinct mode of motion, or is it merely a modification of electricity, heat, or nerve-force? The evidence, so far as it goes, suggests that we have here to do with a psycho-physical medium—a bridge between mind and matter. That the will can direct the odic current is admitted by all who have practised the mesmeric passes. The fluid obeys the mental intention as an arm obeys it. Here, then, at the very threshold of our inquiry, we encounter a fact which should give pause to the dogmatic materialist: a physical force which is, in some manner not yet understood, subject to the conscious volition of a human being, and which can pass from one organism to another, producing therein the most profound physiological and psychological changes.

I cannot leave this subject without recording my conviction that the odic investigations of Reichenbach constitute one of the most important and most unjustly neglected chapters in the history of science. I call upon the younger generation of physicists, who are not yet case-hardened in the prejudices of a mechanical philosophy, to take up this inquiry where the Baron left it. Let them use the spectroscope, the bolometer, the photographic plate, and every refinement of modern instrumentation, to detect and measure the radiations of the living body. I venture to prophesy that, when this is done, the odic force will be found to be the parent of a numerous family of phenomena, of which mesmerism, table-turning, and the séance-room manifestations are the legitimate, though as yet the boisterous, offspring.

### **CHAPTER II.**

#### **MESMERIC CLAIRVOYANCE AND COMMUNITY OF SENSATION.**

We now advance from the general fact of the magnetic influence to its most startling and significant manifestation—the apparent transference of the sensory functions from their material organs to some other region of the body, or even to an external point in space. The phenomenon known as “clairvoyance”—clear-seeing—has been attested by so many competent witnesses that it is no longer possible for the candid mind to dismiss it as an imposture or an illusion. I am perfectly aware that the majority of medical men still regard it with contemptuous incredulity; but I would remind them that the same men, thirty years ago, refused to believe in the stethoscope, and denounced the use of anaesthetics as a violation of the divine decree.

The simplest form of mesmeric clairvoyance is that in which the subject, placed in the trance condition, is able to read a book, or to describe an object, when the eyes are so completely bandaged that no ray of light can reach the retina. I have myself performed this experiment times without number. I take a strip of black velvet, fold it into a thick pad, place it over the eyes of the entranced subject, and then bind it tightly with a silk handkerchief, passing the folds over the ears and knotting them at the back of the head. I then take a volume from my shelf, open it at random, and hold it behind the subject’s head, or against the pit of the stomach, or even at the back of the knee. In a successful case, the subject will read aloud, fluently and correctly, from a page which he has never seen with his waking eyes. He will describe the illustrations, the style of type, and even the marginal notes pencilled by a previous reader. The phenomenon is precisely the same, whether the book be printed in English, French, or Latin—a fact which shows that it is not the mere thought of the operator that is perceived, for the operator may be ignorant of the language in question.

But the transference is not confined to the sense of sight. I have known sensitives who, when a tuning-fork is struck behind their backs, can hear the note and name its pitch, though their ears are stopped with wax and cotton. Others, when a substance—salt, sugar, pepper—is placed upon the palm of the operator’s hand, will instantly taste the flavour in their own mouths. The phenomenon of *community of sensation* is even more remarkable. If the operator pricks his hand with a needle, the sensitive starts and cries out that he feels a sharp pain in the corresponding part of his own body. I once magnetised a young clerk in my employ, and, having induced the trance, I took a pinch of snuff. The youth immediately sneezed violently, and complained that his nostrils were full of a pungent dust. He had not seen me take the snuff, and his eyes were bandaged. When the trance was dissolved, he remembered nothing, and was much astonished to find his handkerchief stained with the moisture of the sneeze.

The most celebrated case of mesmeric clairvoyance on record is that of Mademoiselle Adèle Pigeaire, the daughter of a Montpellier physician, whose marvellous powers were examined, in 1837, by a commission of the Royal Academy of Medicine in Paris. The report, which was drawn up by a sceptic, M. Dubois d’Amiens, and which was subsequently suppressed under pressure of professional ridicule, admitted that the girl, with her eyes sealed by a plaster of pitch and gauze, was able to read, to play at cards, to describe objects placed in a box, and even to name the colours of skeins of silk selected by the commissioners in an adjoining room. The phenomena were declared to be “incontestable,” and the commission pronounced itself unable to explain them by any known physiological law.

I am well aware that the critic will object that all such performances are explicable by hyperaesthesia of the ordinary senses—an abnormal acuteness of vision or hearing, which enables the subject to detect clues that escape the grosser perceptions of the bystanders. There is no doubt that hyperaesthesia does exist, and that it accounts for a certain proportion of the reported marvels. But hyperaesthesia will not explain how a youth can read a book pressed against the soles of his feet, or how a woman can distinguish the colours of objects placed in a closed drawer in an adjoining chamber. To meet such cases, the materialist is driven to suppose a collusion between the sensitive and the operator, or a training of the memory so prodigious as to be itself a wonder. I have taken every precaution known to me to exclude fraud. I have employed sensitives who were total strangers to me; I have used my own books, my own rooms, my own locks and keys; and I have varied the conditions so as to render deliberate trickery a moral impossibility. The phenomena persist.

What, then, is the explanation? The most philosophical hypothesis, in my judgement, is that which supposes a temporary and partial dislocation of the sentient principle—the “soul,” or “consciousness,” or whatever we choose to call it—from its accustomed habitation in the brain. The experiments of Professor William James, of Harvard, and the luminous speculations of Mr. Frederic Myers, of Cambridge, have accustomed us to the notion that the human personality is a far more complex and far more extendable entity than the naive materialism of the schools allows. Beneath the threshold of our ordinary waking consciousness, there lies a vast sub-liminal region, the boundaries of which we can only dimly conjecture. In the mesmeric trance, the threshold is lowered, and this sub-liminal self emerges, displaying faculties—telepathy, clairvoyance, premonition—which, in the normal state, are only exhibited in fugitive and uncertain glimpses. The sensitive does not see with the pit of the stomach or the soles of the feet; his consciousness simply extends to those parts, and apprehends the impressions made upon them by the surrounding ether. The book is not a material object; it is a complex of vibrations. The sub-liminal self, which is native to the world of vibrations, reads it directly, without the intermediation of the optical lens.

I do not offer this hypothesis as a dogma. I offer it as the most convenient working formula which the present state of our knowledge affords. It has the advantage of connecting the facts of clairvoyance with the wider body of psychical phenomena, and it refrains from invoking any agency which is not already known, in its rudiments, to normal psychology. The sub-liminal self is only an extension of that “unconscious cerebration” which Sir William Hamilton and Dr. Carpenter have made familiar to every student of the mind. If we can accept the possibility of unconscious mentation—and no psychologist of standing now denies it—we have taken the first step toward the conception of a consciousness that works beneath, and independently of, the brain and the senses. The mesmeric trance is the experimental proof that such a consciousness exists.

### **CHAPTER III.**

#### **THE PHENOMENA OF THE SÉANCE: PHYSICAL MANIFESTATIONS.**

We now approach that branch of our subject which has excited, more than any other, the mingled curiosity and derision of the general public—the physical phenomena of the séance-room. I refer to those movements of ponderable objects, those mysterious rappings and knockings, those levitations and elongations, which have been so persistently associated with the presence of certain individuals called mediums. It is my duty to state, with all the emphasis at my command, that a large proportion of these phenomena are imposture, and that the spiritualistic movement has been, from its very inception, encumbered by a parasitic growth of charlatans. But when every allowance has been made for fraud, for mal-observation, and for the hysteria of credulity, there remains a solid core of fact which, in my judgement, it would be a greater miracle to deny than to accept.

The history of modern Spiritualism is usually dated from the 31st of March, 1848, when the Fox family, of Hydesville, New York, discovered that mysterious raps occurred in their presence, and that these raps appeared to be intelligent, answering questions by a simple alphabetic code. The subsequent career of the Fox sisters—their public exhibitions, their confessions, and their recantations—forms a sad and sordid chapter which I have no desire to re-open. I will only remark that a confession obtained under pressure, and afterwards retracted, is not worth the paper on which it is written, and that the Hydesville phenomena were corroborated, in their essential features, by scores of independent observers in every civilised country. The raps are a trivial phenomenon in themselves; their importance lies in the fact that they were the first wave of a tide which has swept over the world, compelling attention to a whole class of occurrences which the materialism of the eighteenth century had dismissed as nursery tales.

I shall describe, as concisely as I may, a typical series of manifestations such as I have myself witnessed, under conditions that precluded the hypothesis of trickery. I was one of a circle of eight persons who met, in the winter of 1876, at the residence of a gentleman in Belgravia, for the purpose of sittings with Mr. Daniel Dunglas Home. Mr. Home, the most celebrated physical medium of our age, has submitted his powers to the examination of emperors, men of science, and sceptical journalists, and has never, in a long career, been detected in any fraud that would account for the phenomena occurring in his presence. The circle, on the evening in question, sat around a heavy mahogany dining-table, the legs and castors of which were plainly visible to all. Our hands were placed, lightly and openly, upon its upper surface. The room was illuminated by a gas chandelier, the light of which was ample for the purpose of observation, though it was partially shaded by a ground-glass globe. We had been seated for perhaps a quarter of an hour, engaged in quiet conversation, when a faint, tremulous vibration ran through the table, as though a current of electricity were passing through its substance. This was succeeded by a series of sharp, distinct raps, which appeared to come from the interior of the wood. Mr. Home requested us to call for a tune, and the raps immediately beat out the rhythm of “The Blue Bells of Scotland” with perfect accuracy. At the suggestion of a member of the circle, an alphabet was called, and the raps spelled out a message which purported to come from a deceased relative of one of the sitters—a message containing information known only to that gentleman and his departed friend.

But the raps, however curious, are the least wonderful of the mediumistic phenomena. After some twenty minutes, the table began to sway and tilt, and finally, without any person touching it except by the tips of the fingers resting upon its edge, it rose bodily into the air to a height of at least eighteen inches from the carpet. It remained suspended for several seconds, and then descended gently to its place. I examined the position of the feet of the sitters, and satisfied myself that no mechanical contrivance was in operation. The table was subsequently weighed, and found to be upwards of one hundred and twenty pounds. I am a man of ordinary muscular strength, and I could not have raised it by one hand applied to its edge; yet it rose under conditions which made it physically impossible for any one of the eight persons present to have lifted it by muscular force alone.

Later in the evening, an accordion, which had been placed upon the floor at Mr. Home’s request, was raised into the air and played a plaintive melody, while the medium’s hands were held, one by me and one by my neighbour, at a distance of at least two feet from the instrument. I saw the keys of the accordion move up and down as though pressed by invisible fingers. Still more striking was the levitation of the medium himself. Mr. Home, a slender man of middle stature, rose from his chair, floated horizontally in the air, and passed out of the window—which had been opened to admit him—and in again by the window of the adjoining room, which was likewise open, at a height of some seventy feet from the street. This feat, I am aware, sounds like the wildest fiction of an Eastern romance; but it was witnessed by three persons of unimpeachable veracity, whose joint testimony I am bound to accept, unless I am prepared to abandon the principle that human evidence can ever establish a fact.

I will not multiply instances. My purpose is not to overwhelm the reader with narratives of marvels, but to indicate the character of the evidence upon which the scientific claim of physical mediumship is based. The force which manifests itself in these movements has been christened by Mr. Crookes the “psychic force,” and by M. Thury, of Geneva, the “ectenic force.” It behaves, in many respects, like a mechanical energy. It exerts a measurable pressure; it can be applied to the raising of weights; it sets objects in vibration; and it can produce sounds by percussion. Yet it is, in its origin, distinctly vital and volitional. It emanates from the organism of the medium, and its direction appears to be guided, not by the conscious will of the medium, but by an intelligence which often gives proof of knowledge surpassing that of any person in the room. Here we have the great crux. A force that is directed by intelligence is, by all mechanical analogies, a living force. If that intelligence be not the conscious intelligence of the medium—and often it demonstrably is not—we are compelled to seek its source in some other centre of consciousness, whether the subliminal self of the medium, the collective minds of the sitters, or the discarnate spirits which Spiritualism assumes. This question I shall discuss more fully in a later chapter.

### **CHAPTER IV.**

#### **TRANCE-SPEAKING AND AUTOMATIC WRITING.**

The physical manifestations of the séance, astonishing though they are, do not, in my opinion, constitute the most significant chapter of psychical inquiry. The motions of tables and the levitations of mediums affect the material world, and are therefore, in a sense, continuous with the familiar operations of mechanics. But when we pass to the consideration of trance-speaking and automatic writing, we enter the domain of pure psychology. We are confronted with the strange spectacle of a human being whose organs of speech or of writing are commanded by an intelligence which disclaims identity with that of the entranced person, which frequently exhibits a character and a knowledge foreign to the medium’s waking self, and which often professes to be the discarnate spirit of a deceased man or woman.

The phenomenon of trance-speaking is as old as history. The Delphic Pythia, the Hebrew prophet, the whirling dervish, the inspired sibyl of every age and clime—all testify to the universality of a state in which the ordinary consciousness is replaced by a secondary personality, often gifted with a dignity of utterance and a sweep of vision denied to the normal mind. In the modern Spiritualistic movement, this phenomenon has been exhibited, with every variety of moral and intellectual elevation, by hundreds of mediums. The most notable English exemplar was, beyond all question, the Reverend William Stainton Moses, a clergyman of the Church of England and a graduate of Exeter College, Oxford. Mr. Stainton Moses possessed a mediumship of remarkable purity and power. His physical phenomena—levitations, lights, scents—were abundant; but his chief title to fame rests upon the series of communications known as “The Imperator Band,” which were delivered through him in trance, and afterwards published under the title of *Spirit Teachings*.

The controls of Mr. Stainton Moses purported to be a group of advanced spirits, headed by one who signed himself “Imperator,” and who claimed to have lived, in his earth-life, as a prophet of the Hebrew people. The teachings which they gave were of a lofty ethical and theological character, far surpassing, in their philosophical grasp and their literary finish, anything that the medium could produce in his normal state. I had the privilege of sitting with Mr. Stainton Moses on three occasions. The procedure was always the same. The medium would sit quietly in his chair, and, after a few minutes of silent abstraction, his breathing would become stertorous, a slight convulsive tremor would pass through his frame, and he would rise to his feet, his countenance transfigured with an expression of august serenity. Then, in a voice quite unlike his ordinary conversational tones—a voice that seemed to issue from the depths of his chest, with a resonant and oracular quality—he would deliver a discourse, fluent, coherent, and sustained, upon some theological or moral topic. The address would be punctuated by pauses, as though the speaker were listening to a dictation which we could not hear. When the address was concluded, the medium would sink back into his chair, and, after a brief interval, recover his normal consciousness, entirely unaware of what had occurred.

I have examined the question of Mr. Stainton Moses’s normal intellectual attainments with some care. He was a man of sound but not extraordinary education, a diligent parish priest, with no pretensions to scholarship in Hebrew or in the history of religions. Yet the “Imperator” communications exhibit an intimate acquaintance with the philosophies of the Neo-Platonists, the mysteries of Isis and Mithra, and the intricacies of Christian dogma, which would have done credit to a professor of divinity. The handwriting of the automatic scripts which accompanied the spoken addresses was wholly different from that of the medium, and presented characteristics which a skilled graphologist would attribute to a person of different sex and temperament. I do not pretend to assert dogmatically that these communications proceeded from the spirits of the departed. I do assert, however, that the hypothesis of secondary personality, or of unconscious cerebration on the part of the medium, is strained to the breaking-point when it attempts to account for the mass and quality of the material which Mr. Stainton Moses produced.

The phenomenon of automatic writing is even more widespread than that of trance-speaking, and it has the advantage of leaving a permanent record which can be subjected to critical analysis. The instrument is simple: a pencil, a sheet of paper, and a hand willing to surrender its volition. The writer—I speak from personal experience—sits at a table, holding the pencil lightly, with the point resting on the paper. For a time nothing occurs. Then a faint, involuntary twitch agitates the hand; the pencil makes an aimless stroke, and then another. Gradually the movements become more decided, and words are traced upon the paper—words which the writer does not consciously compose, and which often express thoughts alien to his own convictions. The writing may proceed at a rapid pace, filling page after page, while the writer’s conscious attention is occupied with conversation or with some indifferent book.

I have myself, for the past ten years, been the recipient of a series of automatic scripts which, if they be the product of my own mind, prove that I possess a sub-liminal intellect considerably more acute than my waking intelligence. The communications are written in a hand which my wife assures me is quite unlike my ordinary script—a sharp, upright, Italian hand, while my natural writing is a sprawling, backward-sloping scrawl. The style is terse, epigrammatic, and occasionally Latinised, whereas my own prose is diffuse and Saxon. The “Intelligences” who write—for so they claim to be—give their names, and exhibit traits of character which are consistently maintained over long periods. One of them, who signs himself “Zacharias,” is fond of a dry, scholastic irony; another, “Lydia,” writes in a more emotional and hortatory vein. The information which they impart is frequently of a kind that I am sure I never acquired by any normal process. For instance, “Zacharias” once dictated a passage in mediaeval Latin, which I afterwards found, upon inquiry, to be a quotation from a treatise by Robert Grosseteste, a work which I had never read nor even seen.

The explanation of these phenomena is the central problem of psychical science. The materialist, true to his system, will urge that the whole performance is the work of the writer’s own brain, dissociated, as it were, from the upper consciousness, and endowed with a parasitic life. There is no doubt that a great deal of automatic script is precisely that—the rubbish of the mind’s lumber-room, thrown up by the whirlwind of the subliminal activity. But there is a residue, and a large residue, which this hypothesis will not cover. When a script contains information demonstrably unknown to the writer, and when that information is subsequently verified by independent research, the unconscious-cerebration theory falls to the ground. We are then thrown back upon two alternatives: either the script is the product of telepathic influence from some living mind, or it emanates from an intelligence independent of the living—that is to say, from a spirit. The former alternative may explain some cases, but it multiplies difficulties in others. The latter is the simplest, the most comprehensive, and, to my thinking, the most probable. But I do not dogmatise. I merely record the fact, and leave the reader to form his own conclusion.

### **CHAPTER V.**

#### **MATERIALISATIONS AND THE MYSTERY OF ECTOPLASM.**

Among all the manifestations of the séance-room, none is more calculated to stir the imagination, and none has been the subject of more acrimonious controversy, than the alleged formation of visible, tangible, and apparently living human figures out of a nebulous substance which has been named “ectoplasm.” I approach this subject with a full consciousness of the demands which it makes upon the credence of the reader, and with a sincere resolve to state nothing which I have not either observed with my own eyes or received upon testimony that would be accepted in a court of justice.

The history of ectoplasmic materialisation may be said to begin with the experiments of Professor William Crookes, F.R.S., upon the mediumship of Miss Florence Cook. The details were published in 1874, in the *Quarterly Journal of Science*, and they produced a sensation which has not yet subsided. Miss Cook, a young lady of sixteen, was a private medium of excellent reputation. In the presence of a small circle of investigators, and under conditions of strict control, she produced a full-length female figure, which walked, talked, and allowed itself to be handled, and which claimed to be the spirit of one “Katie King,” who had lived upon earth some two centuries previously. Professor Crookes, a chemist of world-wide fame and a practised experimenter, saw this figure simultaneously with the entranced medium, touched it, measured its pulse, photographed it by the light of a magnesium lamp, and declared that it was, to all appearance, a living woman, with a heart, lungs, and circulation of its own. The features of “Katie King” were not those of Miss Cook; the figure was taller, its complexion darker, its hair and its demeanour altogether different. Yet, upon the dissolving of the form, the substance of which it was composed appeared to be re-absorbed into the body of the medium, leaving her in a state of extreme exhaustion.

Since the time of the Crookes-Cook experiments, the phenomenon of materialisation has been repeated, under varying conditions, in many parts of the world. I have myself witnessed it, on six separate occasions, in the company of witnesses whose honour I hold above suspicion. The procedure is generally as follows. The medium is placed in a cabinet—a small enclosure formed by a curtain drawn across a corner of the room—and is secured by bonds of tape or cord, the ends of which are sealed to the chair or to the floor. The light is reduced to a dim red glow, sufficient to distinguish forms and movements, but not sufficient to permit the minute scrutiny of details. After a period of waiting, during which hymns are usually sung, the curtain parts, and a figure emerges—sometimes a face only, sometimes a hand or an arm, sometimes a full-length form robed in white drapery. The figure walks about the room, touches the sitters, speaks in a whisper or a low voice, and after a longer or shorter interval, returns to the cabinet, where it appears to melt away into the body of the medium.

I am well aware of the objections that will be urged. It will be said that the dim light is a cloak for imposture; that the bonds are easily slipped; that the drapery is smuggled into the cabinet beforehand; and that the whole performance is a piece of vulgar masquerade. I do not deny that in many cases these strictures are just. The annals of Spiritualism are disfigured by the exposure of fraudulent materialisations, from the notorious confessions of the Misses Fox to the detection of “spirit” hands made of painted muslin. But because some bank-notes are forged, do we therefore refuse to accept any bank-note as genuine? The logic that would condemn all mediums on account of the proved frauds of a few is the logic of the nursery, not of the laboratory.

In the sittings to which I have alluded, every precaution known to me was taken to exclude trickery. The medium was searched by a committee of ladies before entering the cabinet. The drapery used was provided by myself, and was locked in a trunk until the moment of the séance. The cord with which the medium was bound was of my own purchase, and the seals bore an engraved die which I had in my pocket. The light, though subdued, was still sufficient for me to recognise the faces of the sitters, and to see that the form which emerged was not the medium, whose breathing I could distinctly hear within the cabinet. On one occasion, I was permitted to grasp the hand of the materialised figure. It was warm, soft, and apparently solid; I felt the bones through the flesh, and the pulse beat under my thumb. Yet, when I endeavoured to retain my hold, the hand seemed to melt from my grasp, and I found myself holding a fragment of the white drapery, which, in a few seconds, dissolved into a damp, gelatinous film upon my palm, and then vanished entirely, leaving no trace.

What is this ectoplasm? The word itself was coined by M. Charles Richet, the distinguished French physiologist, and signifies simply “exteriorised substance.” It is described, by those who have handled it, as a whitish, viscous matter, resembling damp muslin or the white of an egg, which exudes from the body of the medium—usually from the mouth, the ears, or the skin—and which can be moulded, by some unknown agency, into the semblance of human limbs and features. Under a strong light, it recoils and is re-absorbed with startling rapidity. I have seen it, under a faint phosphorescent glow, streaming from the side of the medium like a ribbon of moonlight, coiling and uncoiling, and finally shaping itself into a hand that beckoned to the sitters. The substance is cold to the touch, and has a slightly saline odour. Its analysis, so far as it has been attempted, reveals a composition akin to protoplasm, with a large admixture of water and salts.

The theory which attributes these formations to the spirits of the dead is, no doubt, the one that most readily suggests itself. The figures themselves claim to be such spirits, and they often give evidences of identity—names, dates, personal memories—which are afterwards verified. But there is another hypothesis, which I think deserves serious consideration. It is that the ectoplasmic figure is not an independent spirit, but a temporary projection and animation of the medium’s own sub-liminal consciousness. The “phantom” is, on this view, a thought-form—a living embodiment of the medium’s unconscious memories, desires, and telepathic impressions, built up out of the plastic substance of the body. The fact that the materialised figure so often resembles a deceased person known to one of the sitters may be due to a telepathic reading of the sitter’s memories, rather than to the actual presence of the dead. I do not pronounce between these two interpretations. I only urge that the phenomenon itself is a fact of Nature, and that its investigation promises to throw a flood of light upon the relation between mind and matter.

### **CHAPTER VI.**

#### **THOUGHT-TRANSFERENCE AND MENTAL TELEGRAPHY.**

We now come to a class of phenomena which, more than any other, has succeeded in winning the grudging attention of the scientific world. The transference of thought from one mind to another, without the intermediary of the recognized senses, has been so abundantly demonstrated by the experiments of the Society for Psychical Research that it is no longer possible for an informed man to deny its occurrence. The term “telepathy,” coined by Mr. Frederic Myers, has passed into the current vocabulary, and the fact which it designates is accepted, on the evidence, by psychologists of the eminence of Professor William James and the late Dr. G. Stanley Hall.

The experimental method is, in its essence, simple. Two persons, whom we will call the agent and the percipient, are placed in separate rooms, or even in separate houses, at such a distance that no collusion or unconscious whispering can occur. The agent is given a card, a drawing, a number, or a word, and is requested to concentrate his attention upon it. The percipient, who has no knowledge of what has been chosen, endeavours to reproduce the impression that he receives. The results, when collated, show a percentage of successes which is far beyond what the calculus of probabilities would allow as chance. In the trials conducted by Professor and Mrs. Henry Sidgwick, at Cambridge, the percipients correctly guessed the suit of a playing-card in a proportion that would have occurred by accident only once in millions of trials. In the famous experiments with the Creery family, at Liverpool, the younger daughter was able, when awake, to name words and numbers thought of by the investigators with an accuracy that was mathematically conclusive.

I have myself conducted a long series of telepathic experiments, and I can corroborate the principal findings of the Society. My method was as follows. I prepared a set of fifty diagrams, each drawn in Indian ink upon a white card—simple figures such as a circle, a cross, a triangle, a crescent. I then sat in my study, with the doors closed, and concentrated my gaze upon one of the cards, selected at random by a disinterested third party. The percipient, a young woman of my acquaintance, was seated in the drawing-room, two floors below, with a pencil and a block of blank cards. At a signal given by an electric bell, she would draw whatever impression came into her mind. When the series was complete, I compared the originals with the reproductions. In no less than forty-two cases out of the fifty, the reproduction bore a resemblance to the original which was far too close to be attributed to chance. In eight cases, the reproduction was a duplicate, line for line, of the diagram that I had been contemplating. The distance between us, in this series, was not great; but I have since obtained successes over a distance of three miles, the agent and the percipient being at separate houses, and the signal being given by a pre-arranged clock-time.

The theory of brain-waves, which has been advanced by certain physicists, supposes that the thinking brain emits an ethereal undulation, analogous to the Hertzian waves of wireless telegraphy, and that this undulation, impinging upon the brain of the percipient, sets up a corresponding vibration. The hypothesis is tempting, but it labours under serious difficulties. The intensity of an undulation decreases as the square of the distance, yet thought-transference appears to be as effective at a hundred miles as at a hundred yards. Moreover, the phenomena of telepathy often exhibit a selectivity, an addressing of the message to a particular percipient, which is more suggestive of a voluntary act than of a mere physical radiation. The agent *thinks* of a certain person, and that person, and no other, receives the impression. This element of volition and intelligence is the rock upon which all purely mechanical theories of telepathy have hitherto split.

A still more remarkable branch of the subject is the transference of emotion and of organic sensation. I have recorded a case in which an agent, suddenly seized with an attack of violent toothache while sitting in his club in London, was found, at the same hour, to have produced a sympathetic pain in the jaw of his wife, who was at the time staying in Brighton, and who had no expectation of such an occurrence. The wife, a perfectly healthy woman, was compelled to send for a dentist, who could discover no local cause for the pain. The paroxysm lasted exactly as long as the husband’s attack, and ceased abruptly when the latter had his tooth extracted. Such cases, which might be multiplied indefinitely, point to a substratum of consciousness common to two or more individuals—a transliminal self, as it were, which is not confined within the narrow walls of the skull, but which pervades, like an interpenetrating ether, the whole circle of human sympathies.

The practical applications of thought-transference are as yet in their infancy. I foresee a day when the mental telegraph will be cultivated as systematically as the electric telegraph, and when a man will communicate with his distant friends, not by the slow and clumsy medium of words, but by the instantaneous projection of his thoughts. The experimental foundations of such a science are already laid. It is the duty of the present generation to build upon them, with patience, with precision, and with a resolute disregard of the ridicule of those who mistake their own ignorance for the limits of the possible.

### **CHAPTER VII.**

#### **PHRENOLOGY AND THE LOCALISATION OF PSYCHICAL FACULTIES.**

It may appear strange to the reader that a treatise upon the Borderland sciences should include a chapter upon phrenology, a system which is commonly supposed to be no more than a vulgar empiricism for reading character by the bumps of the head. Yet the phrenological hypothesis, as enunciated by Dr. Franz Joseph Gall and his disciple Spurzheim, is intimately connected with the subject of our inquiry. For phrenology assumes, first, that the brain is the organ of the mind; secondly, that the mind is not a single indivisible entity, but a collection of distinct faculties, each of which has its own cerebral seat; and thirdly, that the development of these faculties can be estimated, with a fair degree of accuracy, by the external configuration of the skull. If these postulates be admitted—and modern neurology, with its localisation of speech, sight, and motion, has confirmed the essential principle—then we have a key to the understanding of many psychical phenomena which would otherwise remain dark.

The phreno-mesmeric experiments, which were the wonder of the forties and fifties, prove, to my mind, the substantial truth of the Gallian system. The procedure was as follows. A sensitive was thrown into the mesmeric trance. The operator then placed his finger upon a particular region of the subject’s scalp, corresponding, according to the phrenological chart, to a given faculty—say, the organ of Combativeness, situated just behind the ear. Instantly, the passive subject would clench his fists, knit his brows, and assume an attitude of pugnacious defiance. If the operator shifted his finger to the organ of Benevolence, the subject’s countenance would relax into a smile, and he would begin to distribute imaginary alms with gestures of the tenderest compassion. The transition was as instantaneous and as inevitable as the striking of a note upon a pianoforte.

I have witnessed this phenomenon in the person of a young Edinburgh mechanic who was mesmerised by Dr. James Braid. The subject was a lad of eighteen, entirely ignorant of phrenology, and incapable of simulating the precise shades of emotional expression which he displayed. When the organ of Veneration was touched, he sank upon his knees and clasped his hands in an attitude of prayer, his face suffused with an expression of adoring awe. When the organ of Mirthfulness was stimulated, he broke into a peal of uncontrollable laughter, which lasted as long as the finger remained upon the spot. The experiment was varied by blindfolding the operator, and allowing an independent observer to indicate, by a silent sign, the organ to be touched; the results were the same. The conclusion is inevitable that certain localised areas of the brain are functionally connected with specific emotional and intellectual states, and that these states can be artificially excited by the magnetic influence of the operator, acting through the skull.

The bearing of this upon our general subject is twofold. In the first place, it demonstrates the existence of a psycho-physical mechanism of extraordinary delicacy, which can be played upon by the will of another person. In the second place, it suggests that many of the phenomena of the séance—the sudden changes of mood, the impersonations of different “controls,” the apparent possession by foreign intelligences—may be due to a temporary excitation or inhibition of the cerebral organs of the medium. I do not say that all trance-personations are reducible to phrenology; but I do say that phrenology offers a valuable clue to the *modus operandi* of the subliminal mind, and that no student of the Borderland can afford to neglect it.

### **CHAPTER VIII.**

#### **APPARITIONS OF THE LIVING AND THE DEAD.**

The collection and analysis of ghost-stories is a task which, until recently, was left to the compiler of chap-books and the teller of Christmas tales. It is the glory of the Society for Psychical Research to have raised this despised department of human testimony to the dignity of a systematic inquiry. The monumental work *Phantasms of the Living*, compiled by Messrs. Gurney, Myers, and Podmore, and published in 1886, contains the records of more than seven hundred cases in which an apparition of a person was seen at or about the moment of his death, by witnesses who had no previous knowledge of the event, and whose testimony was verified by independent documentary evidence. I have myself been a contributor to that volume, and I can vouch for the scrupulous care with which the evidence was sifted.

The typical case runs as follows. A man in robust health is seized, at a distance from his home, with a sudden and fatal malady, or meets with a fatal accident. At the hour of his death, an apparition of the man appears to his wife, his mother, or his intimate friend, in circumstances which preclude the hypothesis of expectation or anxiety. The figure is generally silent, and often wears the clothes which the man was actually wearing at the moment of the crisis. In a number of cases, the apparition has been seen, not by one person only, but by several persons simultaneously—a fact which disposes of the theory of a merely subjective hallucination. In other cases, the apparition has been seen by a person who was not aware that the original was in any danger, and who only learned of the death after a lapse of days or weeks.

I will relate one case which came under my own cognizance, and which I hold to be as well attested as any fact in history. In the winter of 1882, I was visited by a young barrister of the Inner Temple, Mr. A——, who told me the following story. His father, a retired officer living in Devonshire, had died suddenly of heart-failure on the 15th of November. At the moment of death, which was certified by the physician as occurring at eleven o’clock in the forenoon, the barrister, who was walking in the Temple Gardens, saw his father’s figure standing upon the gravel path before him. The figure wore a shooting-coat and a deer-stalker cap, and carried a walking-stick—the very dress in which, as the son afterwards learned, the old gentleman had been walking in his garden half an hour before the seizure. The figure looked at the son with a smile of recognition, raised the stick in a gesture of farewell, and then faded gradually, as a mist dissolves in the sun. The son, who had received no previous intimation of illness, was so impressed that he noted the exact time by his watch, and, on returning to his chambers, wrote a letter to his father, which was intercepted by the telegram announcing the death. The times corresponded to the minute. I have examined the letter, the telegram, the physician’s certificate, and the independent testimony of a friend who was walking with the barrister at the moment of the vision, and who, though he did not see the figure, observed the sudden pallor and agitation of his companion. I am prepared to affirm, upon oath, that the facts are as I have stated them, and that I can find no natural explanation that will cover them.

The theory of the apparition is a matter of speculative philosophy. The spiritistic explanation, that the soul of the dying man projects a phantom of itself to the place where its affections are centred, is, to my mind, the most straightforward and the most consonant with the universal testimony of mankind. The telepathic theory, which supposes that the dying brain transmits an impulse to the brain of the percipient, and that this impulse creates a hallucinatory image, is a possible alternative, but it fails to account for the cases in which the apparition is seen by more than one person, or in which it communicates information that was not in the mind of the dying man. The hypothesis of a world of “phantasmal” images, a sort of astral gallery in which every intense thought or emotion creates a picture that can be perceived by sensitive minds, has been advanced by Mr. Myers and others. It is a splendid and suggestive conception, but it awaits the confirmation of further research. For the present, I am content to register the facts, and to leave the superstructure to the architects of the future.

### **CHAPTER IX.**

#### **THE PSYCHIC FORCE: EXPERIMENTAL DEMONSTRATIONS.**

It has been objected, by critics who have not taken the trouble to examine the evidence, that the physical phenomena of mediumship are incapable of measurement, and that therefore they lie outside the pale of exact science. The objection is unfounded. The psychic force, like every other force, manifests itself by the motion of matter, and motion can be measured. Professor Crookes, in his classical experiments with Mr. Home, employed a spring-balance to measure the increase and decrease of weight which accompanied the phenomena of levitation. He found that a board, one end of which rested upon a weighing-machine, would register a variation of several pounds when the medium’s hands were placed upon it—a variation which could be controlled by the request of the experimenter, and which sometimes exceeded the entire weight of the medium himself. I have repeated this experiment with a table, using a steelyard of precision, and have obtained results which confirm those of Professor Crookes in every particular.

The ectenic force, as M. Thury has shown, behaves in many respects like a mechanical energy. It exerts a thrust which can be measured by the dynamometer. It overcomes the inertia of ponderable bodies. It sets objects in rotation, and the speed of rotation can be timed. In a series of experiments conducted in my own laboratory, a light wicker arm-chair, placed upon a sheet of glass to eliminate friction, was moved across the floor, in the presence of a non-professional medium, without the touch of any person. The distance traversed was three feet; the time occupied was nine seconds; the weight of the chair was eleven pounds. The force required to produce this movement, calculated by the formula *F = ma*, is small but perfectly definite—about a quarter of an ounce. I do not pretend that this force is a new creation of energy. It is, in all probability, a transformation of the vital energy of the medium, just as the steam-engine transforms the chemical energy of coal. But the mode of its transmission, and the intelligence that guides it, are facts of an order wholly different from anything in the mechanical philosophy.

The galvanometer has also been employed, with some success, to detect the emanations of the human body in states of psychical excitation. I have constructed a delicate astatic galvanometer, the terminals of which are connected with two copper plates, placed in a solution of zinc sulphate. When the medium places his hands upon the plates, a current is observed to flow, deflecting the needle by several degrees. The direction of the current varies with the medium’s mental state. In a condition of passive relaxation, the needle is steady; under the excitement of trance or of the “power,” as the Spiritualists call it, the needle swings violently, and the deflection may be maintained for minutes together. I have not yet succeeded in calibrating this current with sufficient precision to deduce quantitative laws; but the fact of its existence is established, and it opens a line of investigation which, I venture to think, is pregnant with results of the highest moment.

The philosophic conclusion to which these experiments point is that the human organism, in certain exceptional conditions, is capable of generating and projecting a force which is intermediate between the mechanical and the mental. It is a force, because it does work; it is a mental force, because it obeys the direction of an intelligence. The old Cartesian dualism, which divided the universe into two utterly disparate substances—thought and extension—is here confronted with a phenomenon that partakes of both natures. The psychic force is the bridge over the abyss that separates mind from matter. It is the tangible evidence that the will is not a mere spectator of the physical drama, but an actor in it, capable of moving, under certain conditions, the very weights and levers of the material world.

### **CHAPTER X.**

#### **THE PHILOSOPHY OF THE BORDERLAND: A SYNTHESIS.**

We have traversed a wide and varied territory, from the odic flame of the magnet to the apparition of the dying man. It remains for us to gather up the threads of our inquiry, and to ask what general conclusions are warranted by the evidence which we have passed in review.

The first and most fundamental conclusion is that the human mind is not the prisoner of the body which it appears to be in ordinary life. The facts of mesmerism, clairvoyance, telepathy, and the séance-room indicate that consciousness can transcend the limits of the organism, can perceive without the senses, and can act upon matter without the mediation of muscle. This conclusion does not rest upon the authority of any single experiment or any single witness; it rests upon the convergent testimony of thousands of observers, in every civilised country, over a period of a hundred years. To reject such a mass of evidence, on the *a priori* ground that it contradicts the known laws of Nature, is to beg the very question at issue. The laws of Nature are not a closed book; they are generalisations from observed facts. If new facts are observed, the laws must be enlarged to include them.

The second conclusion is that the universe contains, interpenetrating the world of matter, a world of mind-stuff, of which the individual human consciousness is a temporary and partial differentiation. This world of mind-stuff is the medium in which telepathic impressions are propagated, in which the odic force operates, and in which, if I interpret the phenomena aright, the personalities of the departed continue to subsist after the dissolution of the physical frame. The evidence for personal survival is, to my mind, cumulative and convincing, though I do not pretend that it amounts to a mathematical demonstration. The communications received through trance-speakers and automatic writers, the apparitions of the dead, the phenomena of materialisation—all these point, with varying degrees of probability, toward the hypothesis that death is not the end of consciousness, but the transition to a larger and freer mode of being.

The third conclusion is one of a more practical and ethical character. If it be true that our thoughts and emotions are not locked within the privacy of our own breasts, but radiate from us like the light of a lamp, affecting for good or ill the minds of those about us, then the responsibility of each individual is enormously increased. A base thought, a malignant desire, is not a private sin, barren of consequence; it is a polluting wave, which spreads through the mental atmosphere, and contributes its share to the sum of human misery. Conversely, a noble aspiration, a loving prayer, is a positive force which heals and uplifts. The Borderland sciences, properly understood, are the foundation of a new ethic, more rigorous and more inspiring than any which the materialist philosophy could ever furnish.

I am aware that in writing thus I lay myself open to the charge of mysticism. To that charge I reply that the mystical interpretation of the universe is the only one that does justice to the whole of the facts. The materialist sees one half of the picture, and calls it the whole; the spiritualist, if he be uncritical, sees the other half, and falls into extravagances which discredit his cause. The true philosopher is he who sees both halves, and who strives, however imperfectly, to trace the connections that unite them. The world of matter and the world of spirit are not two hostile empires; they are the outer and the inner aspects of one reality. The Borderland is the meeting-place of the two, and it is there that the most pregnant discoveries of the future will be made.

In bringing this volume to a close, I cannot do better than quote the words of that prince of spiritual philosophers, Mr. Henry More, the Cambridge Platonist, who wrote in 1659: “That the Soul of Man is a substance distinct from the Body, and that it survives the death of the latter, is a truth not only revealed by Religion, but demonstrated by the manifold phenomena of Nature, which the dull eyes of the Atheist refuse to see. Let us then, with humility and with courage, pursue the investigation of those higher faculties which the Creator has implanted in us, assured that in so doing we are not prying into forbidden mysteries, but fulfilling the highest duty of reasonable beings—the duty of seeking to know the Truth.”

To that noble utterance I subscribe with all my heart. The task which I set before me in these pages is now accomplished, as far as the limitations of my ability permit. I have sought to present, in a scientific spirit, the principal facts of the Borderland sciences, and to indicate the theoretical conclusions to which they seem to me to point. The work is incomplete—I am painfully aware of its deficiencies—but it may serve, perhaps, as a sign-post at the entrance of a path which, in the coming century, will be trodden by abler feet than mine. The dawn is grey, and the shapes that move in it are uncertain; but the dawn is the promise of the day.

### **APPENDIX.**

#### **SELECTED CASES WITH CORROBORATIVE EVIDENCE.**

*[The following cases have been selected from a much larger collection. In every instance, the names of the witnesses are suppressed for obvious reasons, but the original documents, signed and attested, are deposited with the Secretary of the Society for Psychical Research, and are open to the inspection of any qualified inquirer.]*

#### **CASE I.—Mesmeric Clairvoyance.**

In the month of May, 1880, I magnetised a young woman, Miss C. D——, a patient at the Edinburgh Infirmary, who had never been hypnotised before. The trance was profound. I bandaged her eyes in the manner described in Chapter II, and placed in her hand a sealed envelope containing a lock of hair which had been cut, unknown to her, from the head of a friend. She immediately exclaimed, “This is the hair of my friend, Margaret S——. I see her now; she is sitting in a room with a green wall-paper, writing a letter at a desk by the window. She is wearing a blue dress with white spots.” The description was minutely accurate. The friend, upon inquiry, confirmed that she was sitting in such a room, at such a desk, wearing the dress described, at the exact hour of the experiment. The letter which she was writing was received by the post on the following morning.

#### **CASE II.—Table Levitation.**

On the 12th of January, 1881, a circle of six persons, including myself, assembled at my house in Moray Place. The medium, a private lady of unimpeachable character, sat with us. The table was a round mahogany centre-table, weighing, as subsequently determined, one hundred and thirteen pounds. The hands of all present were placed, palms downward, on its surface. The light was from a gas chandelier, and was bright enough for me to see the faces of the company distinctly. After ten minutes, the table tilted, and then rose, with a slow and steady motion, to a height of two feet from the floor. It remained suspended for eight seconds by my watch. At the moment of suspension, I stooped and looked under the table. The feet of all the sitters were drawn back, and no mechanical support was visible. The table descended gently, and no one was hurt.

#### **CASE III.—Telepathic Impression.**

On the night of the 3rd of September, 1883, I was seated in my study, engaged in reading. At half-past ten, I was seized with a sudden and overpowering conviction that a near relative, residing in London, was in great peril. The impression was so vivid that I noted the time in my pocket-book, and wrote a short memorandum of the sensation. The next morning I received a telegram informing me that the relative in question had been run over by a cab in Piccadilly at precisely half-past ten on the previous night, and was lying in St. George’s Hospital in a dangerous condition. The times were subsequently verified by the hospital admission-book. I had no reason whatever to expect any accident to the person concerned.

#### **CASE IV.—A Collective Apparition.**

In the autumn of 1884, a party of four persons—two gentlemen and two ladies—were walking in the garden of a country-house in Perthshire. It was broad daylight, about three o’clock in the afternoon. Suddenly they all saw, standing upon the lawn at a distance of some twenty yards, the figure of a man in an old-fashioned military uniform, wearing a three-cornered hat. The figure remained visible for perhaps thirty seconds, and then faded slowly, as if a mist had risen from the ground. All four witnesses were perfectly sober, in good health, and disbelievers in ghosts. Inquiry elicited that the house had been the seat of a Jacobite family, and that an ancestor of the proprietor had been killed in the rising of ‘45, wearing the uniform of a Highland regiment. The witnesses had no previous knowledge of this fact.
